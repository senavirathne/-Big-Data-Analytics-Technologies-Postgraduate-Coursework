## 5. Literature Review

### 5.1 Enterprise data governance, Data Mesh and regulatory lifecycle management

Centralised data lakes and warehouses remain valuable because they consolidate storage, analytical processing and governance around common infrastructure. This can simplify cross-domain joins, standardise controls and provide economies of scale. Their organisational model, however, often makes a central data team responsible for understanding every source, resolving every semantic question and approving every change. As the number and variety of producers increase, that team can become a delivery bottleneck, while centrally stored data may still lack an accountable owner or the domain context required for safe reuse. Data lakes can therefore become collections of technically accessible but poorly understood data, whereas warehouse schema changes may create broad downstream effects. The evidence for these limitations is primarily implementation experience and architectural analysis rather than controlled comparison: Wider, Verma and Akhtar (2023) derive design guidance from a Data Mesh platform implementation, while Almaslukh *et al.* (2024) evaluate a proof of concept. These studies support the diagnosis but do not establish that decentralisation will outperform a warehouse or lake in every organisation.

Data Mesh changes the operating model by assigning domain teams responsibility for data products while retaining a shared self-service platform and federated governance. A data product should expose discoverable data through defined interfaces, together with semantics, quality expectations, ownership and lifecycle information. Wider, Verma and Akhtar (2023) describe input, output and control ports, service-level objectives, catalogue integration and automated lifecycle management as practical elements of this model. This distribution of responsibility can reduce dependence on a central delivery team and place quality decisions nearer to subject-matter expertise. Nevertheless, Data Mesh is not simply a physical decomposition of a lake or warehouse. Without common interoperability standards, identities, metadata and policy controls, decentralisation may reproduce silos at domain level. A viable design therefore decentralises product decisions while centralising reusable platform capabilities and the minimum rules needed for interoperability, privacy and auditability.

Data Contracts make those boundaries machine-readable. A contract can define schema and semantic meaning, quality thresholds, service objectives, permitted uses, access conditions, provenance and retention, as well as the responsibilities of producers and consumers when an interface changes. Wider, Jarmul and Akhtar (2024) propose contracts containing upstream sharing expectations and downstream assertions, with tests executed during continuous integration and delivery and rechecked as products are composed. This is a promising mechanism for turning federated policy into executable checks. Its effectiveness nevertheless depends on complete dependency information: a local contract cannot demonstrate system-wide compliance when an unrecorded transformation or data copy sits between producer and consumer. Their contribution is an architectural proposal rather than a production-scale trial, so it shows how automation could work, not that contracts eliminate governance failures.

Regulatory obligations have to be enforced across the entire pipeline lifecycle. At ingestion, an organisation should identify the data category, data subject, collection purpose, lawful basis or applicable permission, source, owner, permitted consumers and a retention period or criterion. Article 5 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) establishes purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation, security and accountability; Articles 25 and 32 extend these principles into design/default choices and technical and organisational security measures (European Parliament and Council, 2016). The California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), codified in the California Civil Code, similarly requires notice of categories and purposes, limits collection, use, retention and sharing to what is reasonably necessary and proportionate, and addresses downstream contractual restrictions and security in section 1798.100 (State of California, n.d.-a). The specific catalogue fields proposed above are an architectural interpretation of those duties, not a schema prescribed by either law.

During processing, each transformation should preserve source-to-output lineage and record the responsible job, policy context, contract version and quality result. During sharing, access decisions should use the recorded purpose, classification and consumer identity, and external transfers should remain traceable to applicable contracts. During archiving, retention schedules and legal holds need to be distinguishable so that inactive data does not become an uncontrolled secondary store. Finally, Article 17 GDPR provides a right to erasure subject to stated conditions and exceptions, while Article 30 requires records of processing activities (European Parliament and Council, 2016). CCPA section 1798.105 requires qualifying deletion requests to be propagated to service providers, contractors and relevant third parties, also subject to exceptions (State of California, n.d.-b). End-to-end lineage is therefore important for locating replicas, derived tables, features, exports, backups and consumers affected by a correction or deletion. A catalogue can provide evidence and trigger workflows, but it cannot determine a lawful basis, resolve every exception or guarantee that a downstream system actually deletes data. Regulatory compliance still requires legal interpretation, accountable human decisions and enforcement in storage and execution systems.

Apache Atlas offers a technically relevant governance substrate through extensible entity types, classifications, search and lineage. Its classifications can represent concepts such as personally identifiable information, expiry status and data-quality state, and can propagate along lineage; its integration with Apache Ranger can translate metadata into access controls and masking policies (Apache Software Foundation, 2023). The Hive hook also captures table and column lineage for supported operations (Apache Software Foundation, 2020). In a governed pipeline, these capabilities can support impact analysis, policy targeting, audit evidence and identification of assets affected by a data-subject request. Classification propagation must still be configured carefully: a rule that blindly transfers a sensitive label can over-restrict an aggregate, whereas failure to propagate through custom transformations can expose derived sensitive data.

OpenMetadata similarly records upstream and downstream relationships among tables, pipelines and dashboards, deriving some lineage from parsed queries and allowing manual additions where automation is unavailable (OpenMetadata, n.d.-a). Its glossary supports governed terminology, review and version history, helping domains align business meaning while retaining ownership of their products (OpenMetadata, n.d.-b). OpenMetadata also combines role- and attribute-based policies to control access to metadata resources and operations (OpenMetadata, n.d.-c). This makes it useful for contract discovery, change-impact analysis and lifecycle evidence. Neither catalogue guarantees complete lineage. Custom code, opaque software-as-a-service platforms, manual file transfers and stale connectors can create gaps; identity mappings and metadata freshness can also diverge from the operational system. Moreover, catalogue access control does not necessarily enforce access to the underlying data: that normally remains the responsibility of an identity provider, query engine, storage service or integrated policy system. Atlas and OpenMetadata should therefore be treated as control-plane components whose assertions are tested against runtime events, rather than as automatic compliance mechanisms.

Overall, the strongest contribution of Data Mesh is organisational ownership, while Data Contracts provide testable interfaces and catalogues provide shared visibility. These mechanisms are mutually dependent. Domain autonomy without contracts makes interoperability fragile; contracts without lineage cannot establish the effect of composition; and lineage without runtime enforcement is descriptive rather than protective. A defensible enterprise architecture combines domain-owned products with centrally supplied identity, metadata, contract testing, lineage capture, policy enforcement and lifecycle workflows.

### 5.2 Quantum and generative-AI directions for big-data engineering

Quantum machine learning (QML) and quantum-enhanced database processing are research directions rather than general solutions to big-data scale. Some database decisions, including multiple-query optimisation, join ordering, scheduling and index design, can contain NP-hard combinatorial subproblems. NP-hardness alone does not imply a useful quantum speed-up: an advantage must be demonstrated for a defined problem, input representation, algorithm and cost model, including state preparation, measurements and classical post-processing.

Haug, Self and Kim (2023) reduce the quantum-computation requirement of a randomised-measurement quantum-kernel method to linear scaling with dataset size and demonstrate image classification using noisy IBM hardware and distributed execution. The classical reconstruction of the kernel remains quadratic in dataset size, and the broader method can still scale exponentially with the number of qubits. This is a proven result for a particular kernel construction, not evidence that QML accelerates arbitrary analytical pipelines. Jerbi *et al.* (2023) likewise show, for specified learning tasks, that data-reuploading models can require exponentially fewer qubits than linear quantum models and fewer samples than associated kernel methods. Their theoretical and numerical separation is model- and task-specific; it does not establish an unrestricted quantum advantage over mature classical machine-learning systems.

Database evidence is even narrower. Fankhauser *et al.* (2023) formulate multiple-query optimisation for a gate-based quantum computer and test it using both simulation and real hardware. They report qubit efficiency close to 99 per cent, almost twice that of the compared state-of-the-art quantum-annealing implementation. This is a substantive prototype result, but the hardware experiments address small instances and do not show superior cost, latency or reliability for production databases. Noise, limited qubit counts, repeated measurements and the overhead of encoding a changing workload remain barriers. Quantum optimisation could eventually propose candidates for query-plan selection or resource scheduling, but a classical cost model and execution test would still need to verify the result.

Quantum implications for multidimensional indexing divide into two different problems. Trummer and Venturelli (2024) map the NP-hard selection of which conventional database indexes to materialise onto a D-Wave quantum annealer; this optimises physical design but does not replace an R-tree, vector index or other query-time access method. Zardini, Blanzieri and Pastorello (2024) implement quantum Euclidean-distance estimation for *k*-nearest-neighbour search, a relevant primitive for high-dimensional proximity queries. Their claimed time advantage assumes quantum random-access memory; without that assumption there is no true time-complexity advantage, and finite measurements reduce accuracy. Neither study demonstrates a multidimensional quantum index with transactional updates, exact range-query semantics and favourable end-to-end cost. Quantum assistance is therefore evidenced for narrow index-selection and distance-estimation subproblems, while production multidimensional indexing remains classical.

Cryptographic signatures face a security-migration implication rather than a processing speed-up. Signatures authenticate data contracts, transformation code, lineage events, model artefacts and checkpoints, but NIST warns that future quantum computers threaten current signature standards and has standardised ML-DSA and SLH-DSA as quantum-resistant alternatives (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2024a; 2024b). A long-lived data platform should consequently be crypto-agile: record the signature algorithm and key version, preserve verification metadata, and plan re-signing or archival protection before vulnerable schemes are retired. Quantum processing provides no reason to bypass signature verification or to claim that a “quantum signature” improves database execution. A defensible hybrid system verifies signed inputs and outputs in the classical governance plane before and after invoking any quantum accelerator.

Large language models (LLMs) present a nearer-term opportunity because they can generate SQL, Spark or Flink transformations and configuration candidates from natural-language requirements. The safe pattern is closed-loop generation: the model proposes code; deterministic tools parse, compile and type-check it; schema and Data Contract rules constrain it; a sandbox executes it against representative data; security, quality, performance and regression tests score the result; and failures are returned to the model for bounded revision. Only a validated candidate is promoted, with human approval for high-impact changes. The model is therefore a proposal engine outside the trusted execution core, not an autonomous authority.

Text-to-SQL research demonstrates parts of this loop. SQLPrompt generates diverse candidates and uses execution-based consistency to select an answer with little labelled data (Sun *et al.*, 2023). DART-SQL rewrites questions and iteratively refines generated queries using database content and execution results; across four benchmarks, Mao *et al.* (2024) report average execution-accuracy improvements of 12.41 and 5.38 per cent for the two evaluated baselines. These are peer-reviewed benchmark results, but neither execution success nor agreement among candidates proves semantic correctness. A query can run and still join the wrong keys, omit a business filter, disclose restricted fields or return a plausible wrong result. Enterprise text-to-SQL should therefore ground prompts in approved schemas and glossary terms, use read-only sandboxes with row and time limits, enforce column- and row-level policies, and compare results with invariants or approved examples.

The same control pattern can support predictive optimisation. Giannakouris and Trummer (2025) present λ-Tune, which generates complete database configurations, selects among a small candidate set, and bounds evaluation cost as a function of the optimal runtime; experiments on PostgreSQL and MySQL show greater robustness than the compared automated-tuning baselines. This supports an LLM-propose, empirically benchmark-and-evaluate loop for database configuration, but not an untested claim that an LLM can safely optimise Spark or Flink plans. Extending the loop to partition strategies, resource settings or skew responses remains an architectural inference. Proposals should be constrained by hard limits, benchmarked against the current plan and rolled back when service objectives deteriorate.

Self-healing extract-transform-load (ETL) pipelines are also plausible but should be defined conservatively. Monitoring can identify a schema-contract violation, quality drift, failed dependency or service-level breach; an LLM can then explain the incident and propose a patch. The patch should be compiled and run in an isolated environment, replayed against representative or quarantined inputs, checked for data-quality invariants and lineage changes, and tested for idempotent backfill and rollback. Automatic promotion is appropriate only for narrowly pre-authorised changes that pass deterministic controls; semantic transformations and privacy-affecting changes require review. Sun *et al.* (2023) and Mao *et al.* (2024) show that execution feedback can improve generated SQL, but extending this result to self-repairing distributed ETL is forward-looking synthesis rather than experimentally proven performance.

Nondeterminism is the central integration risk. In 829 code-generation tasks, Ouyang *et al.* (2025) find that identical ChatGPT prompts frequently produce divergent programs and that a zero-temperature setting reduces, but does not remove, variation. Although this experiment concerns general code generation rather than Spark, Flink or SQL specifically, it provides direct evidence that a repeated prompt is not a reproducible build process. Every proposed artefact should consequently be treated as untrusted and versioned with the prompt, model and configuration, schema and catalogue snapshot, retrieved context, candidate set, selection rationale, test outputs and a content hash or signature.

Spark and Flink should supply a controlled execution boundary, but they are not intrinsically deterministic in every circumstance: unordered partitions, event timing, floating-point aggregation, external calls and user-defined functions can change results. Reproducibility therefore depends on deterministic contracts and configuration, explicit ordering where required, stable dependencies, bounded event-time rules, idempotent writes, checkpointing and replayable inputs. A robust loop generates several candidates, canonicalises and validates them, selects one through recorded tests, signs the approved artefact and executes that exact version rather than regenerating it at runtime. Runtime metrics and lineage then feed the next optimisation cycle without silently changing production code. Exactly-once processing and idempotence should be verified as configured system properties, not assumed from an engine label.

The literature consequently supports a layered conclusion. Narrow QML scaling results, small-hardware multiple-query optimisation, quantum index-selection prototypes, quantum distance estimation and execution-guided text-to-SQL improvements are demonstrated findings. Production multidimensional quantum indexes, LLM-led Spark/Flink optimisation and self-healing ETL remain reasoned research directions. All should sit behind the same governance plane developed in Section 5.1: Data Contracts constrain inputs and outputs; catalogues record ownership and lineage; quantum-resistant signatures protect approved artefacts; deterministic validation gates deployment; and human accountability remains explicit. This combination permits experimentation without presenting probabilistic generation or immature quantum processing as a substitute for regulatory, semantic or operational control.
